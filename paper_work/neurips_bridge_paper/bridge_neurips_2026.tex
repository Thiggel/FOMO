\begin{abstract}
Self supervised pretraining inherits the sampling bias of its source data. Most remedies for long tailed self supervised learning are model centric. They modify the loss, the schedule, or the optimization dynamics while leaving the training distribution fixed. We present \method, a data centric alternative that alternates pretraining, mean $k$NN OOD scoring, and targeted image generation with a frozen diffusion model. The final method replaces a simpler extreme tail selector with a histogram based mode window that targets the modal shoulder of the OOD distribution and avoids the noisy far tail. Across ImageNet-100-LT, CIFAR-10-LT, CIFAR-100-LT, PASS, and DiffusionDB source pretraining, \method\ achieves the best average linear probe transfer among the evaluated methods. On ImageNet-100-LT source pretraining, it improves the seven task average over imbalanced SimCLR by 3.05 points, and across the five source regimes its gain over source matched SimCLR ranges from 2.41 to 4.89 points. Ablations show that Stable Diffusion 3 is the strongest generator, SimCLR is the strongest SSL objective inside the repair loop, and the mode window selector is a better replacement for pure top tail sampling.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Self supervised learning has become a standard way to learn transferable visual representations from unlabeled images. Strong methods now span contrastive learning, masked prediction, and self distillation~\citep{chen2020simple,he2020momentum,he2022masked,DINO,oquab2023dinov2}. Their success, however, still depends on the geometry of the source data. When the source corpus is long tailed or otherwise skewed, the learned representation devotes most of its capacity to dense regions and leaves sparse regions weakly modeled. This is a data problem as much as it is an optimization problem, and it can reduce downstream transfer on both in domain and fine grained targets.

Most prior attempts to address long tailed SSL are model centric. Temperature Schedules reshapes the optimization trajectory of contrastive learning over time~\citep{ts}. SDCLR introduces a self competitor to emphasize harder and rarer samples~\citep{sdclr}. Other work adds external OOD data or frequency aware training heuristics to the SSL loop~\citep{colt,fassl}. These methods are important baselines, but they still treat the source distribution as mostly fixed.

We study the complementary direction and ask whether the source dataset itself can be repaired while SSL training proceeds. We propose \method, a cyclic data repair loop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bridge-overview}. After each pretraining stage, we embed the current training set, score each image by mean $k$NN distance, select a sparse but still semantically recoverable region of the representation space, and generate new training images for that region with a frozen diffusion model. The next cycle trains on the repaired dataset. The encoder therefore reveals where the data manifold is thin, and the generated images densify those regions before the next round of learning.

Two aspects of the final design are central to the present study. First, the main method no longer selects the most extreme OOD samples. Instead it builds a histogram over OOD distances and allocates a fixed sampling budget around the modal region of that histogram. This skips the super OOD tail, which often contains noisy or weakly useful images. The pure tail rule is retained as an explicit ablation. Second, the empirical scope is substantially broader. In addition to ImageNet-100-LT, CIFAR-10-LT, and CIFAR-100-LT, we evaluate web scale unlabeled source data from PASS and DiffusionDB. BRIDGE is best on the seven task average in all five source regimes that we study.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it introduces a data centric SSL loop that repairs sparse representation regions with frozen diffusion augmentation rather than changing the SSL loss. Second, it replaces pure tail selection with a histogram based mode window that better targets semantically recoverable sparse regions. Third, it provides a broad empirical study across five source regimes, seven downstream tasks, three seeds, competitive long tail SSL baselines, and five targeted ablation groups.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Self supervised visual representation learning.}
Modern vision SSL is dominated by contrastive methods such as SimCLR and MoCo~\citep{chen2020simple,he2020momentum}, self distillation methods such as DINO~\citep{DINO}, and masked prediction methods such as MAE~\citep{he2022masked}. These methods have made unlabeled pretraining routine, but they usually treat the source dataset as fixed and place most of the burden on the objective.

\paragraph{Long tailed and imbalanced SSL.}
Long tailed data changes both optimization and representation geometry. TS addresses this with a schedule over the contrastive temperature~\citep{ts}. SDCLR prunes a self competitor to emphasize hard and tail examples~\citep{sdclr}. FASSL introduces frequency aware SSL training~\citep{fassl}. COLT injects external OOD data into long tail SSL~\citep{colt}. Our setting is different. \method\ does not require class frequencies, does not modify the core SSL loss, and does not require a separate external OOD collection. It instead repairs sparse regions using images generated from the data already being trained on.

\paragraph{OOD scoring and data curation.}
Data centric AI emphasizes that dataset composition can be as important as model design~\citep{whang2023data}. For our setting, the scoring rule must work without labels, density estimation assumptions, or class frequency estimates. Mean deep nearest neighbor distance satisfies these constraints and has been effective for OOD detection in high dimensional representation spaces~\citep{sun2022out}. Our contribution is to use this signal not only for detection, but for targeted repair of underrepresented regions.

\paragraph{Diffusion based augmentation.}
Diffusion models enable high quality image synthesis with strong semantic control~\citep{ho2020denoising,rombach2022highresolution}. Prior long tail work has often used generated images in supervised settings. Our use is different. A frozen image to image diffusion model serves as a repair operator inside an SSL loop, and generation is targeted at sparse representation regions instead of globally sampled prompts.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Setting and repair loop}
Let $D^{(0)} = \{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N_0}$ denote an unlabeled source dataset and let $f_{\theta}$ be an SSL encoder trained with objective $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ssl}}$. \method\ alternates between pretraining and dataset repair over $C$ cycles. At cycle $c$, the current encoder is $f_{\theta^{(c)}}$ and the current source set is $D^{(c)}$.

After training on $D^{(c)}$, we embed every image and compute a non parametric OOD score from mean $k$NN distance,
\begin{equation}
    z_i^{(c)} = f_{\theta^{(c)}}(x_i), \qquad
    d_i^{(c)} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{j=1}^{k} \left\lVert z_i^{(c)} - \pi_j\left(z_i^{(c)}\right) \right\rVert_2^2,
\end{equation}
where $\pi_j\left(z_i^{(c)}\right)$ is the $j$th nearest neighbor of $z_i^{(c)}$ inside the current training set. Large values indicate that an image lies in a sparse part of the learned representation space.

\subsection{Mode window OOD selection}
\label{sec:mode-window}
The old version of our method selected the largest OOD scores directly. That rule is simple but brittle. The farthest samples are often visually noisy, outlying even relative to the sparse regions that are still semantically useful for repair.

Our current selector therefore works in two steps. First, it clips the empirical OOD distribution to its $1\%$ to $99\%$ quantile range and builds a histogram over the clipped values. Let $b^{\star}$ denote the modal bin and let $m^{(c)}$ denote the center of that bin. Second, given an augmentation budget $B$, we select the $B$ samples whose OOD scores are closest to $m^{(c)}$,
\begin{equation}
    S^{(c)} = \operatorname*{arg\,top-}B_i\, -\left| d_i^{(c)} - m^{(c)} \right|.
\end{equation}
This yields a symmetric selection around the densest sparse region rather than a direct grab of the farthest tail. Figure~\ref{fig:mode-window} illustrates the mechanism, and Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:cycle-histories} shows that the OOD shoulder can move substantially across training.

\subsection{Diffusion based repair}
Given the selected set $S^{(c)}$, we generate $G$ synthetic variants per selected image with a frozen image to image diffusion model $g_{\phi}$. In the main method we use Stable Diffusion 3. The generation ablation replaces it with FLUX or with a no generation re population baseline. The repaired source set is
\begin{equation}
    D^{(c+1)} = D^{(c)} \cup \left\{ g_{\phi}(x, \epsilon_j) \mid x \in S^{(c)},\, j = 1, \dots, G \right\}.
\end{equation}
Because the generator is frozen, the learned component remains the SSL encoder. The repair loop is therefore compatible with any SSL objective that can provide embeddings for OOD scoring.

\subsection{Design rationale}
The key choice is that we do not try to densify the most isolated samples at all costs. The farthest tail often contains degenerate crops, artifacts, or images whose semantics are too unstable for reliable image to image augmentation. The histogram mode targets a more useful region. It identifies images that are underrepresented relative to the main body of the representation space, yet still belong to a coherent local neighborhood. In the completed selection ablation, this change improves the full seven task average over the older top tail rule for both linear probe and $k$NN transfer. Uniform sampling remains competitive and even slightly stronger on average in that isolated ablation, so we view the mode window as a principled replacement for extreme tail selection rather than as a universally dominant selector.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\paragraph{Source datasets.}
We pretrain on five unlabeled source regimes. Three are long tailed image classification datasets, namely ImageNet-100-LT, CIFAR-10-LT, and CIFAR-100-LT. Two are web scale unlabeled corpora, namely PASS~\citep{pass} and DiffusionDB~\citep{diffusiondb}. For PASS and DiffusionDB we use 10k example subsets and override the pretraining split to $[1.0, 0.0, 0.0]$, so all sampled images are used for SSL pretraining.

\paragraph{Downstream evaluation.}
We evaluate transfer on seven benchmarks: CIFAR-10-LT, CIFAR-100-LT, Stanford Cars~\citep{cars}, FGVC Aircraft~\citep{aircraft}, Oxford Flowers~\citep{flowers}, Oxford-IIIT Pets~\citep{pets}, and ImageNet-100-LT. The main paper reports linear probe results. Full linear probe and $k$NN tables are deferred to the appendix.

\paragraph{Training protocol.}
All reported numbers are mean $\pm$ standard deviation over three seeds. The main backbone is ResNet-50. Unless noted otherwise, BRIDGE uses $k = 100$ for OOD scoring, selects 500 images per cycle, generates 5 images per selected image, and trains for 5 cycles with 100 epochs per cycle. The cycle ablation changes the number of cycles while adjusting the per cycle budget accordingly. The successful DINO ablation uses two local crops. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} gives additional implementation details, runtime summaries, and asset notes.

\paragraph{Compared methods.}
We compare SimCLR, TS, SDCLR, \method, and two hybrids that combine BRIDGE with TS or SDCLR. These are meaningful comparisons because BRIDGE changes the data while TS and SDCLR change the optimization dynamics.

\paragraph{Ablation protocol.}
All ablations are run on ImageNet-100-LT source pretraining with the same downstream suite and three seed evaluation. Each ablation changes one component at a time. The pretraining ablation swaps SimCLR, MoCo, and DINO. The generation ablation compares Stable Diffusion 3, FLUX, and no generation re population. The selection ablation compares the new mode window rule, the old top tail rule that takes the most OOD examples directly, and uniform sampling. The cycle ablation varies the number of repair cycles. The architecture ablation compares ResNet-18, ResNet-50, ViT-S, and ViT-B.

Table~\ref{tab:main_ablation_selection} compares the new mode window selector, the old top tail rule that directly takes the most OOD examples, and uniform sampling. \section{Limitations}
BRIDGE is more expensive than fixed data SSL because every cycle requires feature extraction, OOD scoring, and image generation. The successful experiments reported here use a specific compute regime based on A100 nodes and 24 hour wall time limits, and Appendix~\ref{app:compute} shows that total project compute is substantial. The PASS and DiffusionDB experiments are limited to 10k subsets for tractability. The main paper also centers the final comparison on ResNet-50, even though the appendix includes broader architecture ablations. Finally, the submission does not benchmark newer methods that appeared after the experimental campaign was completed.

\section{Conclusion}
The experiments support a simple conclusion. Long tail SSL is not only an optimization problem. It is also a data geometry problem. By repeatedly identifying sparse representation regions and repairing them with frozen diffusion augmentation, BRIDGE improves average transfer across conventional long tailed sources and across two web scale unlabeled sources. The new mode window selector matters because it replaces an overly literal extreme tail rule with a more stable target region. Within the completed experimental protocol, BRIDGE is the strongest average transfer method across all five source regimes that we study.

\section{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{app:implementation}
\paragraph{Core training configuration.}
The completed main runs use ResNet-50 encoders. Most baseline, BRIDGE, and source regime comparisons use 5 cycles with 100 epochs per cycle. The default BRIDGE configuration uses mean $k$NN distance with $k = 100$, selects 500 samples per cycle, and generates 5 images per selected sample. The default optimizer is AdamW with weight decay $10^{-4}$, and method specific learning rates and temperatures follow the completed job configurations.

\paragraph{OOD scoring and selection.}
Features are extracted without feature normalization and nearest neighbor distances are computed with FAISS using squared $\ell_2$ distance. The mode window selector clips the empirical OOD distribution to the 1st to 99th percentile range, builds a histogram with the code level auto bin heuristic, and selects the examples closest to the modal bin center.

\paragraph{Generators.}
The main method uses Stable Diffusion 3 in image to image mode. The generation ablation replaces it with FLUX or with a no generation re population baseline. In the completed PASS and DiffusionDB runs we increase the SD3 batch size to 12 to fit the 24 hour Slurm limit.

\paragraph{Source data specifics.}
PASS and DiffusionDB use \texttt{train[:10000]} with pretraining split override \texttt{[1.0, 0.0, 0.0]}. This keeps the source data budget aligned across methods while avoiding unnecessary pretraining validation overhead. The long tailed ImageNet-100, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100 regimes follow the completed job configurations used in the final sweep.

\paragraph{Seeds and reporting.}
All main claims are supported by three seed averages. Tables report mean $\pm$ standard deviation over those seeds. The exact table values are generated from the completed job logs by the local export scripts in \texttt{scripts/export\_paper\_results.py} and \texttt{scripts/generate\_neurips\_paper\_tables.py}.

\section{Compute Resources and Runtime}
\label{app:compute}
Most completed experiments use one node with two NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs and a 24 hour wall time limit. The successful DINO ablation uses four A100 80GB GPUs after reducing the number of local crops from six to two. Over the successful experiments reported in the paper, the aggregate training time is approximately 3022 wall clock hours, or about 6142 GPU hours. This excludes failed runs, timeout runs, cache migration jobs, and other debugging or pilot jobs, so the full project required more compute than the successful totals alone.

Representative mean wall clock times over three seeds are as follows. On ImageNet-100-LT source pretraining, SimCLR takes 16.9 hours and BRIDGE takes 21.8 hours. On PASS, SimCLR takes 10.2 hours and BRIDGE takes 14.9 hours. On DiffusionDB, SimCLR takes 11.6 hours and BRIDGE takes 16.4 hours. The successful DINO ablation takes 16.3 hours on four GPUs in its final configuration.

\section{Broader Impact}
\label{app:impact}
A method that improves representation quality under biased source data can reduce the need for manual relabeling and can make SSL more useful for domains where balanced data is difficult to collect. That is a positive outcome. At the same time, our method relies on large image generators and on web sourced datasets. These carry familiar risks related to license compliance, privacy, and unintended memorization or harmful content. Our experiments stay within existing dataset and model release terms and do not release new generators or scraped image collections. Any future deployment would still require a task specific review of fairness, privacy, and content safety.

\section{Algorithm}
\begin{algorithm}[h]
\caption{BRIDGE training loop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Initialize unlabeled source set $D^{(0)}$, encoder $f_{\theta}$, frozen generator $g_{\phi}$
\FOR{$c = 0, \dots, C-1$}
    \STATE Pretrain $f_{\theta}$ on $D^{(c)}$ for $E_c$ epochs
    \STATE Embed all images in $D^{(c)}$ and compute mean $k$NN distances $\{d_i^{(c)}\}$
    \STATE Clip the distance range, build a histogram, and find the modal bin center $m^{(c)}$
    \STATE Select $B$ images whose OOD distances are closest to $m^{(c)}$
    \STATE Generate $G$ synthetic images per selected image with frozen generator $g_{\phi}$
    \STATE Form $D^{(c+1)}$ by adding the generated images to $D^{(c)}$
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For example, if the contribution is a novel architecture, describing the architecture fully might suffice, or if the contribution is a specific model and empirical evaluation, it may be necessary to either make it possible for others to replicate the model with the same dataset, or provide access to the model. In general. releasing code and data is often one good way to accomplish this, but reproducibility can also be provided via detailed instructions for how to replicate the results, access to a hosted model (e.g., in the case of a large language model), releasing of a model checkpoint, or other means that are appropriate to the research performed.
        \item While NeurIPS does not require releasing code, the conference does require all submissions to provide some reasonable avenue for reproducibility, which may depend on the nature of the contribution. For example
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item If the contribution is primarily a new algorithm, the paper should make it clear how to reproduce that algorithm.
            \item If the contribution is primarily a new model architecture, the paper should describe the architecture clearly and fully.
            \item If the contribution is a new model (e.g., a large language model), then there should either be a way to access this model for reproducing the results or a way to reproduce the model (e.g., with an open-source dataset or instructions for how to construct the dataset).
            \item We recognize that reproducibility may be tricky in some cases, in which case authors are welcome to describe the particular way they provide for reproducibility. In the case of closed-source models, it may be that access to the model is limited in some way (e.g., to registered users), but it should be possible for other researchers to have some path to reproducing or verifying the results.
        \end{enumerate}
    \end{itemize}
