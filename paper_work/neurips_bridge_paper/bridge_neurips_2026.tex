\begin{abstract}
Self-supervised learning inherits the coverage biases of its source data. When an unlabeled corpus is long-tailed or otherwise skewed, representation space becomes dense around common modes and thin where semantic coverage is weak. Loss-level fixes can improve optimization, but they do not directly repair this geometry. We introduce \method, a data-centric self-supervised training loop that alternates between representation learning, mean $k$NN sparsity scoring, and targeted image-to-image diffusion augmentation. Rather than chasing the extreme OOD tail, \method\ targets a mode-centered band inside the upper tail of the OOD-distance distribution and enforces diversity within that band before generation. Across five source regimes, including long-tailed ImageNet-100, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, PASS, and the fully synthetic DiffusionDB, \method\ achieves the best average linear-probe and $k$NN transfer in every regime we study. Relative to source-matched SimCLR, it improves seven-task linear-probe averages by 2.44 to 10.53 points. Ablations show that the strongest configuration combines SimCLR within the repair loop, Stable Diffusion 3 for generation, and an upper-tail mode-window selector that is substantially better than both top-tail and uniform selection on ImageNet-100-LT.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Self-supervised learning is now a standard way to learn transferable visual representations from unlabeled images. Strong methods span contrastive learning, masked prediction, and self-distillation~\citep{chen2020simple,he2020momentum,he2022masked,DINO,oquab2023dinov2}. Yet these methods still inherit coverage biases from the source corpus. Prior work shows that imbalance can hurt self-supervised learning and motivates specialized long-tailed training strategies~\citep{tian2021divide,assran2210hidden,ts,sdclr}. We view one reason as geometric: learned features can concentrate around frequent modes while providing weaker coverage of underrepresented regions. This perspective suggests a complementary response to objective design: repair the source data itself so that sparse semantic regions are revisited during pretraining.

Most prior attempts to address long-tailed SSL are model-centric. Temperature Schedules reshapes the optimization trajectory of contrastive learning over time~\citep{ts}. SDCLR introduces a self-competitor to emphasize harder and rarer samples~\citep{sdclr}. These methods are strong baselines, but they still treat the source distribution as largely fixed.

We study the complementary direction and ask whether we can repair the source data while SSL training proceeds. We propose \method, a cyclic data-repair loop shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bridge-overview}. After each pretraining stage, we embed the current training set, score each image by mean $k$NN distance, select a sparse but still semantically recoverable region of representation space, and generate new training images for that region with a frozen diffusion model. The next cycle then trains on the repaired dataset. In effect, the encoder reveals where the source manifold is thin, and the generator densifies those regions before the next round of learning.

Two aspects of \method\ are central. First, BRIDGE restricts attention to the upper sparse band of the OOD distribution, finds the mode within that band, and then selects a diverse set of anchors around that mode. This avoids the super-OOD tail, which often contains noisy or weakly useful images, while retaining the underrepresented region that is most amenable to repair. In the ablations, we compare this selector against top-tail and uniform alternatives. Second, we evaluate BRIDGE across five distinct source regimes. In addition to ImageNet-100-LT, CIFAR-10-LT, and CIFAR-100-LT, we study web-source unlabeled data drawn from PASS and from the fully synthetic DiffusionDB. Across these five regimes, \method\ is best on the seven-task average and remains strong under both linear-probe and $k$NN transfer.

We make three contributions. First, we introduce a data-centric SSL loop that repairs sparse representation regions with frozen diffusion augmentation instead of modifying the SSL loss. Second, we introduce an upper-tail mode-window rule with diversity-aware subsampling, which targets semantically recoverable sparse regions without drifting into the extreme OOD tail or wasting budget on near-duplicates. Third, we provide a broad empirical study across five source regimes, seven downstream tasks, three seeds, competitive long-tailed SSL baselines, and five targeted ablation groups.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Self-supervised visual representation learning.}
Modern vision SSL is dominated by contrastive methods such as SimCLR and MoCo~\citep{chen2020simple,he2020momentum}, self-distillation methods such as DINO~\citep{DINO}, and masked prediction methods such as MAE~\citep{he2022masked}. These approaches have made unlabeled pretraining routine, but they typically hold the source dataset fixed and place the burden of improvement on the learning objective.

\paragraph{Long-tailed and imbalanced SSL.}
Long-tailed data changes both optimization and representation geometry. TS addresses this through a schedule over the contrastive temperature~\citep{ts}. SDCLR prunes a self-competitor to emphasize hard and tail examples~\citep{sdclr}. We target a different lever. \method\ does not require class frequencies and does not alter the core SSL loss. Instead, we repair sparse regions using images generated from the data already being trained on.

\paragraph{OOD scoring and data curation.}
Data-centric AI emphasizes that dataset composition can matter as much as model design~\citep{whang2023data}. In our setting, the scoring rule must work without labels, density estimation assumptions, or class-frequency estimates. Mean deep nearest-neighbor distance satisfies these constraints and has been effective for OOD detection in high-dimensional representation spaces~\citep{sun2022out}. We use this signal not only to detect sparsity, but to drive targeted repair of underrepresented regions.

\paragraph{Diffusion based augmentation.}
Diffusion models enable high-quality image synthesis with strong semantic control~\citep{ho2020denoising,rombach2022highresolution}. Prior long-tailed work often uses generated images in supervised settings. We instead use a frozen image-to-image diffusion model as a repair operator inside an SSL loop, and we target generation to sparse representation regions rather than to globally sampled prompts.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Setting and repair loop}
Let $D^{(0)} = \{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N_0}$ denote an unlabeled source dataset and let $f_{\theta}$ be an SSL encoder trained with objective $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ssl}}$. \method\ alternates between pretraining and dataset repair over $C$ cycles. At cycle $c$, the current encoder is $f_{\theta^{(c)}}$, the current source set is $D^{(c)} = \{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N^{(c)}}$, and the corresponding embedding set is $Z^{(c)} = \{z_i^{(c)}\}_{i=1}^{N^{(c)}}$.

After training on $D^{(c)}$, we embed every image and compute a nonparametric OOD score from mean $k$NN distance,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    z_i^{(c)} &= f_{\theta^{(c)}}(x_i), \\
    d_i^{(c)} &= \frac{1}{k}\sum_{z \in \mathcal{N}_k\!\left(z_i^{(c)};\, Z^{(c)} \setminus \{z_i^{(c)}\}\right)}
    \bigl\lVert z_i^{(c)} - z \bigr\rVert_2^2,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}_k(z; Z)$ denotes the set of the $k$ nearest neighbors of $z$ in $Z$. Large values of $d_i^{(c)}$ indicate that an image lies in a sparse part of the learned representation space.

\subsection{Mode-window OOD selection}
\label{sec:mode-window}
A natural alternative is to select the largest OOD scores directly. In practice, that rule is unstable. The farthest samples are often artifacts, degenerate crops, or semantically incoherent outliers. Uniform sampling has the opposite failure mode: it spreads budget across the whole dataset, including regions that are already well covered. We instead target a sparse region that is still internally coherent.

Our selector works in three steps. First, it restricts attention to the upper sparse band of the empirical OOD distribution, namely the scores between the $75$th and $99$th percentiles, and builds a histogram over that band. Let $b^{\star}$ denote the modal bin and let $m^{(c)}$ denote the center of that bin. Second, given an augmentation budget $B$ and a candidate-pool multiplier $\alpha$, we form a mode-centered candidate set of size $M = \alpha B$:
\begin{equation}
    C^{(c)} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{C \subseteq U^{(c)},\, |C| = M}
    \sum_{i \in C} \bigl| d_i^{(c)} - m^{(c)} \bigr|,
\end{equation}
where $U^{(c)} = \{i : q_{0.75}^{(c)} \le d_i^{(c)} \le q_{0.99}^{(c)}\}$ and $[N^{(c)}] = \{1, \dots, N^{(c)}\}$. Third, we choose the final anchor set $S^{(c)}$ by greedy farthest-point sampling in embedding space from that candidate pool:
\begin{equation}
    S^{(c)} = \operatorname{FPS}\!\left(C^{(c)}, B; Z^{(c)}\right).
\end{equation}
This keeps selection inside a stable sparse regime while discouraging near-duplicate anchors. In our implementation, $\alpha$ is simply the candidate-pool multiplier, and we set $\alpha = 4$, so the final anchors are chosen from a pool of size $4B$ before diversity filtering. Figure~\ref{fig:cycle-histories} is best read as an illustration of why the selector is adaptive: the upper-tail shoulder shifts across cycles, so a fixed threshold or a pure extreme-tail rule would not track the same semantically useful region over time.

\subsection{Diffusion based repair}
Given the selected set $S^{(c)}$, we generate $G$ synthetic variants per selected image with a frozen image-to-image diffusion model $g_{\phi}$. In the main method we use Stable Diffusion 3. The generation ablation replaces it with FLUX or with a no-generation re-population baseline. We first form the generated set
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \widetilde{D}^{(c)} &= \left\{ g_{\phi}(x_i, \epsilon_j) \,\middle|\, i \in S^{(c)},\, j \in [G] \right\}, \\
        D^{(c+1)} &= D^{(c)} \cup \widetilde{D}^{(c)},
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $[G] = \{1, \dots, G\}$.
Because the generator is frozen, the learned component remains the SSL encoder. The repair loop is therefore compatible with any SSL objective that can provide embeddings for OOD scoring.

\subsection{Design rationale}
The central design choice is that we do not treat all sparse points as equally useful repair targets. The extreme tail is often too unstable for reliable image-to-image generation, whereas fully uniform sampling spends too much budget on regions that already have adequate support. We therefore separate two decisions: where to allocate budget and how to cover that region efficiently. The upper-tail histogram mode identifies a sparse region that remains semantically recoverable, and farthest-point sampling spreads anchors across that region instead of duplicating nearly identical examples. In the selection ablation, this combination improves the seven-task average over both top-tail selection and uniform sampling under linear-probe and $k$NN evaluation.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\paragraph{Source datasets.}
We pretrain on five unlabeled source regimes. Three are long-tailed image classification datasets, namely ImageNet-100-LT, CIFAR-10-LT, and CIFAR-100-LT. We construct these long-tailed sources by applying the same shuffled power-law keep-ratio procedure to the original training split of each dataset, following the ImageNet-LT style setting. On ImageNet-100-LT this yields an ImageNet-LT style head-to-tail range from 1280 images in the largest class to 5 in the smallest class after a random permutation of class identities, and the CIFAR variants use the same keep-ratio construction on their respective training splits. Two additional source regimes are web-source unlabeled corpora, namely PASS~\citep{pass} and DiffusionDB~\citep{diffusiondb}. DiffusionDB is fully synthetic, which makes it a useful stress test for whether BRIDGE helps even when the source corpus already comes from a generative pipeline. For PASS and DiffusionDB we use aligned 10k example subsets.

\paragraph{Downstream evaluation.}
We evaluate transfer on seven benchmarks: CIFAR-10-LT, CIFAR-100-LT, Stanford Cars~\citep{cars}, FGVC Aircraft~\citep{aircraft}, Oxford Flowers~\citep{flowers}, Oxford-IIIT Pets~\citep{pets}, and ImageNet-100-LT. In the main text, we report full linear-probe comparisons, together with $k$NN corroboration for the source-regime comparisons and the combined ablation table. The appendix provides the complete expanded linear-probe and $k$NN tables for every setting.

\paragraph{Training protocol.}
All reported numbers are mean $\pm$ standard deviation over three seeds. The main backbone is ResNet-50. Unless noted otherwise, BRIDGE uses $k = 100$ for OOD scoring, selects 500 images per cycle, generates 5 images per selected image, and trains for 5 cycles with 100 epochs per cycle. The balanced baseline is constructed to match the imbalanced source budget by uniformly removing images from the balanced source until the total number of source images matches the corresponding long-tailed dataset. The cycle ablation changes the number of repair cycles while adjusting the per-cycle budget accordingly. The successful DINO ablation uses two local crops. Because the downstream tasks are heterogeneous, we emphasize per-dataset tables and use the average only as a compact summary statistic. Appendix~\ref{app:implementation} gives additional implementation details, runtime summaries, and asset notes.

\paragraph{Compared methods.}
We compare SimCLR, TS, SDCLR, \method, and two hybrids that combine BRIDGE with TS or SDCLR. These are meaningful comparisons because BRIDGE changes the data while TS and SDCLR change the optimization dynamics.

\paragraph{Ablation protocol.}
All ablations are run on ImageNet-100-LT source pretraining with the same downstream suite and three-seed evaluation. The pretraining ablation swaps SimCLR, MoCo, and DINO. The generation ablation compares Stable Diffusion 3, FLUX, and no-generation re-population of the originally removed data. This is a useful sanity check because it distinguishes the effect of semantically guided generation from the effect of merely increasing the amount of data. The selection ablation compares three upper-tail mode-window ranges, a top-tail rule that takes the most OOD examples directly, and uniform sampling. The cycle ablation varies the number of repair cycles. The architecture ablation compares ResNet-18, ResNet-50, ViT-S, and ViT-B.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Balanced and imbalanced baselines}
Tables~\ref{tab:main_baselines_linear} and~\ref{tab:main_baselines_knn} establish a useful control. On ImageNet-100-LT source pretraining, balanced and imbalanced SimCLR are nearly indistinguishable on the seven-task averages, at 31.1 versus 31.2 for linear probing and 26.2 versus 26.3 for $k$NN. Simple budget-matched balancing therefore does not recover strong transfer on its own. \method\ changes that picture substantially. It raises the seven-task average to 42.3 for linear probing and 33.6 for $k$NN, improving over imbalanced SimCLR by 11.10 and 7.29 points, respectively. The gain is large, broad, and visible on every downstream column. The role of these baseline tables is therefore not to claim that every form of imbalance must always collapse SSL performance. Their role is to show that, in our setting, repairing sparse representation regions is far more effective than changing the source class histogram alone.

\subsection{Comparison across source datasets}
Table~\ref{tab:main_sota_label_sources} reports the three label-derived long-tailed source regimes with all seven downstream tasks shown directly. The main conclusion is not subtle: BRIDGE is the strongest average method in all three regimes, and the gains are substantial. The clearest case is CIFAR-100-LT source pretraining, where the seven-task average rises from 23.8 for source-matched SimCLR to 34.4. On ImageNet-100-LT, BRIDGE improves the average from 39.8 to 42.5. On CIFAR-10-LT, it improves the average from 29.1 to 34.2. Across the three regimes, the pattern is stable: objective-level changes can help selectively, but repairing the source set produces the strongest broad transfer.

\input{tables/main_sota_label_sources}

Table~\ref{tab:main_sota_label_sources_knn} shows that this ranking is not specific to linear probing. Under $k$NN transfer, \method\ remains the strongest average method in all three label-derived regimes, improving over SimCLR from 31.8 to 33.6 on ImageNet-100-LT source pretraining, from 24.0 to 28.7 on CIFAR-10-LT, and from 20.9 to 27.0 on CIFAR-100-LT. This matters because $k$NN removes the learned linear head from the evaluation. The gains therefore reflect changes in feature geometry itself, not only easier linear readout.

For the ImageNet-100-LT target in particular, the gains are not confined to a handful of head classes. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:imagenet100lt-frequency-bins} bins the target classes by the frequency they had in the long-tailed source construction and compares BRIDGE against SimCLR within each bin. Under linear probing, the largest average gain appears in the rarest quartile of classes, while the $k$NN gains remain positive but are more diffuse across quartiles. We therefore treat this as supporting evidence that BRIDGE helps lower-frequency regions of the source distribution, without claiming a perfectly monotone tail-only effect.

Table~\ref{tab:main_sota_web_sources} evaluates the two web-source regimes, which are qualitatively different from the label-derived long-tailed datasets. PASS is a natural web image corpus, while DiffusionDB is fully synthetic. \method\ is again the strongest average method in both settings. On PASS-10k, the seven-task average rises from 36.8 for SimCLR to 39.9 for BRIDGE. On DiffusionDB-10k, it rises from 37.7 to 40.2. The DiffusionDB result is especially informative. Because the source data are already synthetic, the gain cannot be explained only by harvesting more natural web diversity. It instead indicates that the repair loop remains useful even when the source corpus already carries strong generative and stylistic bias.

The same conclusion holds under nonparametric evaluation. Table~\ref{tab:main_sota_web_sources_knn} reports full $k$NN transfer for the two web-source regimes. On PASS-10k, \method\ improves the seven-task $k$NN average from 29.3 to 30.6. On DiffusionDB-10k, it improves the average from 28.2 to 29.9. Together with Table~\ref{tab:main_sota_web_sources}, this shows that the effect is not tied to a learned probe and not restricted to natural image corpora.

\paragraph{SSL objective.}
SimCLR is the strongest objective inside the \method\ loop on every downstream dataset and on the overall average. Its seven-task average is 42.3, compared with 17.8 for MoCo and 15.2 for DINO. The gap is not a single-target effect. It is visible on all seven downstream tasks, and it remains large under $k$NN evaluation in Table~\ref{tab:main_ablation_combined_knn}. In this setting, the repair loop works best with a standard contrastive objective rather than momentum contrast or self-distillation. Practically, this matters because it shows that BRIDGE does not depend on a highly specialized SSL backbone.

\paragraph{Generation mechanism.}
Stable Diffusion 3 is the strongest generator in the repair loop. It reaches a seven-task average of 41.4, compared with 35.0 for FLUX and 32.6 for no-generation re-population. The gain over re-population is therefore 8.81 points, and the gain over FLUX is 6.33 points. This pattern is again broad rather than localized. Stable Diffusion 3 is best on every downstream task in the table, with especially large margins on Flowers, Pets, and ImageNet-100-LT. The ablation therefore supports a specific conclusion: BRIDGE benefits from semantically faithful image-to-image generation, not merely from adding more samples to the source set.

\paragraph{Sample selection.}
The selection block isolates the main design change in the final method. We compare three upper-tail mode-window variants with different percentile ranges, together with a top-tail rule that spends all budget on the most extreme OOD examples and a uniform rule that ignores sparsity entirely. The result is clear. All three diversity-aware mode-window variants are much stronger than top-tail and uniform. The default q75--99 variant reaches a seven-task average of 42.3 for linear probing and 33.6 for $k$NN, compared with 35.5 and 28.4 for uniform and 34.3 and 27.3 for top-tail. The q50--99 and q1--99 variants remain close, which shows that the gain is not brittle to one exact cutoff once selection is restricted to a sparse band and diversified within that band. We therefore use q75--99 as the default because it is the strongest linear-probe setting and tied for the strongest $k$NN average.

\paragraph{Cycle count.}
Performance improves sharply once the repair loop is allowed to run for several rounds, but the best setting in this sweep is 5 cycles rather than the longer alternative that completed reliably. Relative to 2 cycles, the 5-cycle configuration improves the seven-task averages by 7.36 points for linear probing and 5.70 points for $k$NN. It also remains stronger than the 10-cycle setting on every downstream target in the main table. Longer repair schedules therefore do not help monotonically in this regime. Five cycles appear to strike the best balance between repeated source repair and preserving the quality of the pretraining distribution.

\paragraph{Encoder architecture.}
ResNet-50 is the strongest linear-probe backbone overall. It gives the best seven-task average, at 42.3, and leads on six of the seven downstream targets. ViT-B remains best on Flowers, but its overall average is 34.8, far below ResNet-50. The backbone choice still shifts emphasis across targets, yet the repaired-data setting clearly favors ResNet-50 as the default architecture in the main sweep.

\paragraph{$k$NN corroboration.}
Table~\ref{tab:main_ablation_combined_knn} shows the same five ablation blocks under $k$NN transfer, and the main conclusions remain intact. SimCLR remains the strongest SSL objective, Stable Diffusion 3 remains the strongest generator, the upper-tail mode-window variants remain much better than top-tail and uniform selection, and the 5-cycle configuration remains the best cycle setting. The backbone block changes only slightly: ViT-B is strongest on a few individual $k$NN columns, but ResNet-50 still gives the best overall average. This stability matters. It shows that the repaired-data gains are not artifacts of linear-probe fitting and instead reflect consistent changes in neighborhood structure.

\section{Limitations}
The reported PASS and DiffusionDB results use aligned 10k source subsets drawn from larger web-source corpora, so they evaluate \method\ under a fixed source budget rather than at the full corpus scale. We also keep the broad five-regime comparison on one operating point, based on ResNet-50, five repair cycles, and one generation budget, and move sensitivity to architecture and cycle count into targeted ablations. Finally, we evaluate representation transfer through linear probing and $k$NN classification. These are appropriate diagnostics for the data-repair hypothesis, but they do not exhaust the downstream settings in which repaired source data could matter.

\section{Conclusion}
We find that long-tailed SSL is not only an optimization problem. It is also a data-geometry problem. By repeatedly identifying sparse representation regions and repairing them with frozen diffusion augmentation, \method\ improves average transfer across conventional long-tailed sources and across two web-source regimes. The mode-window selector matters because it avoids allocating repair budget to an overly literal extreme OOD tail and instead targets a more stable sparse region. Within our experimental protocol, \method\ is the strongest average transfer method across all five source regimes that we study.

\section{Implementation and Reproducibility}
\label{app:implementation}
\paragraph{Core training configuration.}
The main experiments use ResNet-50 encoders. Most baseline, \method, and source-regime comparisons use 5 cycles with 100 epochs per cycle. The default \method\ configuration uses mean $k$NN distance with $k = 100$, selects 500 samples per cycle, and generates 5 images per selected sample. The default optimizer is AdamW with weight decay $10^{-4}$, and method-specific learning rates and temperatures follow the settings used in each experiment.

\paragraph{OOD scoring and selection.}
We extract features without feature normalization for OOD scoring and compute nearest-neighbor distances with FAISS using squared $\ell_2$ distance. The mode-window selector restricts the OOD distribution to the 75th to 99th percentile range, builds a histogram with the code-level auto-bin heuristic, forms a mode-centered candidate pool of size $4B$, and then applies greedy farthest-point sampling in normalized feature space to select the final $B$ anchors.

\paragraph{Generators.}
The main method uses Stable Diffusion 3 in image-to-image mode. The generation ablation replaces it with FLUX or with a no-generation re-population baseline. In the PASS and DiffusionDB experiments, we increase the SD3 batch size to 12 to fit the 24-hour wall-time limit.

\paragraph{Source data specifics.}
PASS and DiffusionDB use \texttt{train[:10000]} with pretraining split override \texttt{[1.0, 0.0, 0.0]}. This keeps the source data budget aligned across methods while avoiding unnecessary pretraining validation overhead. DiffusionDB is fully synthetic, which is why it serves as a useful source-regime stress test in the main paper. The long-tailed ImageNet-100, CIFAR-10, and CIFAR-100 source regimes use the same shuffled power-law subsampling procedure described in the main text, without the 10k subset restriction used for PASS and DiffusionDB. On ImageNet-100-LT, the resulting class counts range from 1280 to 5; the CIFAR variants use the same keep-ratio template on their own class distributions.

\paragraph{Seeds and reporting.}
All main claims are supported by three seed averages. Tables report mean $\pm$ standard deviation over those seeds. The exact table values are computed directly from the three runs corresponding to each reported setting.

\paragraph{Code availability.}
An anonymous code release accompanying this submission is available at \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/status/FOMO-0488}.

\section{Compute Resources and Runtime}
\label{app:compute}
Most reported experiments use one node with two NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs and a 24-hour wall-time limit. The DINO ablation uses four A100 80GB GPUs after reducing the number of local crops from six to two. Across the successful experiments we report, the aggregate training time is approximately 3022 wall-clock hours, or about 6142 GPU hours. This excludes failed runs, timeout runs, cache migration jobs, and other debugging or pilot jobs, so the full project required more compute than the successful totals alone.

Representative mean wall-clock times over three seeds are as follows. On ImageNet-100-LT source pretraining, SimCLR takes 16.9 hours and BRIDGE takes 21.8 hours. On PASS, SimCLR takes 10.2 hours and BRIDGE takes 14.9 hours. On DiffusionDB, SimCLR takes 11.6 hours and BRIDGE takes 16.4 hours. The successful DINO ablation takes 16.3 hours on four GPUs after reducing the number of local crops from six to two.

\section{Broader Impact}
\label{app:impact}
A method that improves representation quality under biased source data can reduce the need for manual relabeling and can make SSL more useful in domains where balanced data are difficult to collect. That is a positive outcome. At the same time, our method relies on large image generators and on web-sourced datasets. These carry familiar risks related to license compliance, privacy, and unintended memorization or harmful content. Our experiments stay within existing dataset and model release terms and do not release new generators or scraped image collections. Any future deployment would still require a task-specific review of fairness, privacy, and content safety.

\section{Algorithm}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{BRIDGE training loop with mode-window selection}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE Initialize source set $D^{(0)}$, encoder $f_{\theta^{(0)}}$, frozen generator $g_{\phi}$, cycle budgets $\{E_c\}_{c=0}^{C-1}$
\FOR{$c = 0, \dots, C-1$}
    \STATE Train or continue training $f_{\theta^{(c)}}$ on $D^{(c)}$ for $E_c$ epochs
    \STATE Compute embeddings $Z^{(c)} = \{f_{\theta^{(c)}}(x_i) : x_i \in D^{(c)}\}$
    \STATE Compute mean $k$NN scores $\{d_i^{(c)}\}_{i=1}^{N^{(c)}}$ from $Z^{(c)}$
    \STATE Restrict $\{d_i^{(c)}\}$ to the 75th to 99th percentile range and build a histogram
    \STATE Let $m^{(c)}$ be the center of the modal histogram bin inside that upper-tail band
    \STATE Form a candidate set $C^{(c)}$ of size $4B$ whose scores are closest to $m^{(c)}$
    \STATE Select $S^{(c)} \subseteq C^{(c)}$ with $|S^{(c)}| = B$ by greedy farthest-point sampling in embedding space
    \STATE Form $\widetilde{D}^{(c)} = \{g_{\phi}(x_i, \epsilon_j) \mid i \in S^{(c)},\, j \in [G]\}$
    \STATE Update $D^{(c+1)} = D^{(c)} \cup \widetilde{D}^{(c)}$
\ENDFOR
\STATE Return the final encoder and repaired source set
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
